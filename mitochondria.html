Mithocondria, symbiosis and the coolest evolutionary theory

Maria Matias · August 2026 · 11 min read

Prokaryotes, eukaryotes and the beginning of life!

I was, as one does, freaking out over my Biology and (unfortunately) Geology exam and between my incredibly unproductive study sessions, I came across a subject I haven't thought about in months, and which I believe is so cool that you'll have a few pages' worth of me just geeking out all over it! This is none other than what I modestly believe is one of the most interesting concepts in evolutionary history, as well as microbiology.

So, you, dear reader, might be wondering: what is this super amazing theory that deserves such praise? It's none other than endosymbiosis.

Here I'll try to go over what exactly this theory is, what might support it and what might conflict with it, and then offer some of my own reflections and how this might impact our own view of microbiology and evolution. So, onto the juicy stuff!

So, let's start at the beginning. If you haven't already forgotten your 8th grade science class, there are two main classes of cells that compose the domain of life. They present extremely different characteristics, structures, and functioning systems - it's actually wild how much! Prokaryotes - imagine your bacteria - are usually considered simpler forms of life. They don't contain a nucleus, i.e. their genetic information is not nicely organised in a membrane-bounded structure (in fact, they don't have any membrane-bounded structure within them! Only on the outside, separating intracellular from extracellular environments; they just kind of float around in a closed strand of DNA in a zone called the nucleoid! On the other hand, we have eukaryotes; these are much more complex cells: they have a nucleus, where they can much more safely and efficiently guard their genetic information; they also have an extremely complex, quite detailed and quite ubiquitous-in-exams endomembrane system, which means they have tons of little compartments within them, which also increases the efficiency of the cell. Want to know the best? They can be organised in larger systems to create multicellular life! So you, my friend, are just a huge bag of eukaryotic cells! Eukaryotic cells also have other particularly interesting characteristics in some of their organelles, mitochondria and chloroplasts. These organelles are semi-autonomous: they have their own DNA! This will be really important later, so keep that in mind!

As you may imagine, it's estimated that prokaryotic life, being less complex, originated before eukaryotic life! And that, under current evidence, sounds about right! The oldest evidence we have of a prokaryotic organism dates back to about 3.5 billion years ago, and these were estimated to be prokaryontes which obtained their energy from hydrothermal vents in the deep ocean.

But the transition from prokaryotes to eukaryotes was rather slow, as we may imagine: evidence suggests that it took around 1.5-1.7 billion years for life to evolve into the eukaryotic life we know today, and this wasn't linear either. In fact, there's a lot of confusion about when exactly the first eukaryotic life began. The estimates I mentioned earlier are related to what is called the first eukaryotic common ancestor, or FECA, for friends ;). This is the first organism that might have differentiated from its prokaryotic ancestors and which could be identified as a eukaryote, which is much different from the LECA or last eukaryotic common ancestor, the cell or population of cells that presented a fully eukaryotic system and gave origin to the rest of the more complex forms of life we know today. And between these two, almost 1 billion years could have passed (maximum, as you may have seen, there's a lot of debate about timelines, and experts are very uncertain about how organisms have evolved over such a long timeframe)! The time between the two ECAs is (cleverly) called eukaryogenesis. During these beautiful hundreds of millions of years, cells had time to acquire endomembranous systems, a nucleus, a different DNA organisation and the one we are exploring most today: mitochondria.

The origin of mitochondria

Mitochondria are really big deals. They are organelles, i.e components of the cell, that produce the energy necessary for all the metabolic processes essential for their survival. So their appearance in eukaryotic life led to all kinds of benefits; they could now access so much more energy than previously because they had access to aerobic respiration. And with more energy, these cells could develop much more and, eventually, they began to develop multicellular life (although, keep in mind, this is a multi-million-year evolutionary jump!).

Now, we've settled the importance of mitochondria. But they are pretty complex organelles: they have their own circular DNA, a double membrane, and even a different pH than the rest of the cell. How did a eukaryotic cell evolve all of this? The answer is teamwork. Yes, or, as it's most often and accurately called, symbiosis. Symbiosis is the biological relationship between two different organisms that benefits both, so much that one could hardly survive without the other. For example, look no further than your gut! The bacteria that inhabit it are essential for much of your body's regulation and for improving digestion! And, in exchange, they really like the food and protection your body provides them!

And symbiosis seems to have an important part in eukaryotic evolution too! Mitochondria might have evolved in a similar (but slightly different) fashion from what we just described. The currently accepted theory is that mitochondria are themselves reminiscent of prokaryotic cells living inside of eukaryotic cells! (It's nuts! I know! I'll let that sink in for a moment).

Ready to go to the specifics? The endosymbiotic theory states that one cell must have engulfed another prokaryotic, aerobic cell - the most likely candidate is alpha-proteobacteria.

It may have happened for a variety of reasons, either because of a parasitic invasion of the host or due to the capture of the cell, which was able to resist digestion. That engulfment is often called 'initiation'. After initiation, the host cell and the endosymbiont (the one that 'lives' inside it) started to have a cooperative relationship. The host cell had its own extremely effective means of energy production (as aerobic respiration is highly productive), while the endosymbiont had quick access to nutrients and a larger cell to protect it. Very gradually, this relationship began to evolve: there was most probably a special type of horizontal gene transfer involved.[1] The endosymbiotic organism transferred genetic material to its host, which improved the synchronisation of cell division and growth. Eventually, this resulted in a reduction of the endosymbiont's DNA, becoming totally dependent on its host, and thus they fused into organelles, which are part of the host's cell and lost most of their autonomy. We are then left with mitochondria in eukaryotic cells.

But this didn't just happen once… This process most likely occurred several times in evolutionary history! The other very significant candidate is chloroplasts! Already-mithocondria-equipped cells probably started another endosymbiotic relationship, this time around not with aerobic bacteria but with cyanobacteria. And the trades were quite nice: the host had access to a very comfortable source of organic matter, and the endosymbiont had more energy and protection! These organisms may have evolved into algae, and ultimately into the terrestrial plant life we know today.

This also explains the name of the theory: endo- inside and symbiosis, as we have seen, a mutually beneficial relationship. So, a relationship between two organisms, where one lives inside the other, enabling the emergence of extremely complex life.

What about proof?

I don't know about you, but when I first read this theory, I was quite appalled. How could one reach the conclusion that some organelles were the result of cells eating each other up?

Well, back in 1967, a (really, really genius) evolutionary biologist named Lynn Margulis gathered a quite compelling set of evidence that strongly supported this hypothesis. Although she was overlooked, and even judged by her peers for her rather radical ideas at that time, her work was an essential pillar in cell evolution.

So, let's look at some evidence, one by one.

Beginning with the obvious: their shape. If we compare mitochondria with today's simple bacteria, we can identify striking physical similarities: from their shape to their size, the two biological systems seem, at the least, related.

Then, we have DNA: mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own DNA, separate from the cell's own. Moreover, this DNA is circular, not linear, like eukaryotes' but extremely similar to prokaryotes' genetic material. This seems to indicate that they may be related somehow. These organelles also don't have histones, which are proteins present in eukaryotes' DNA, essential for its organisation and compaction but which aren't present in bacteria.

Genomic analysis also indicates that mitochondria and chloroplasts are evolutionarily related to bacteria, much more so than their eukaryotic counterparts, pointing towards a closer relationship between one part of the cell and other organisms but not another! (Now, that's neat).

And endosymbiosis applied outside of evolution is proven to exist; in fact, there are several examples in nature! Paramecium Bursario is a type of protozhoary which became photosynthetic only after associating with microalgae of the Zoochlorella genre. In fact, P. Bursario may become so packed with these algae that it even looks green rather than its usual transparent colour! Both organisms benefit as P. Bursario shares nutrients with the algae, as well as chauffeuring it around to sunlight!

I've just stated some of the most compelling evidence, but many other facts also support this theory, and its number just seems to increase! The type of ribosomes found in chloroplasts and mitochondria are more similar to bacteria than other ribosomes found inside the same cell; some antibiotics affect these organelles and bacteria alike but not the eukaryotic cell itself, or the fact that mitochondria and chloroplasts reproduce by binary fission, much like bacteria: if we remove mitochondria from a cell, it cannot produce new ones, nor will they be present in descendants if tey were to replicate (much like if mitochondria was a cell of its own)!

That sounds compelling! But is it settled? No… Actually, far from that, there is still a lot of uncertainty and shadow lurking beneath this seemingly bullet-proof theory. Like the origin of the prokaryotic cell that gave rise to mitochondria: some genes indicate that are bacteria, but others also show the presence of archaea. This could have happened due to transfer to the nucleus, but most of the mechanisms of that transfer still remain a mystery to the researchers working in this area.

Despite this, it is one of the most accepted and, in fact, sensible theories out there about how such complex organisms like eukaryotes could have appeared. Although rather complex, and maybe even wild (at least for me, so)!

Why this is particularly fascinating.

I'll now let myself a little loose on the philosophy of biology, an area I'm intolerably bad at but fascinated by (which is, arguably, the worst junction to be at in a subject). One of the things that fascinated me the most about this theory is just how different it is from the ideas we see so often about evolutionary theory. There's a lot of presence not only in the scientific community, but also in our day-to-day lives, seeing evolution (maybe even generalising on progress) through a very Darwinian lens. The 'survival of the fittest', the 'adapt or die', which, although one of the main drivers of evolution, shows a more brutal and accidental side of how everything we know came to be. We always see mutations and variation as the starting point of divergence between species, so when I was presented with this theory, I was pleasantly surprised to see cooperation. It's quite interesting to think that one of the key characteristics - aerobic respiration - that allowed organisms to develop into super complex beings came because different cells found that they could better survive by helping one another, rather than a ruthless fight for survival.

And risking falling into the fallacy of humanising a bunch of neuronless cells living a couple of billion years ago, I see some beauty right there. Some encouragement even. It's not just about trying to survive, or at least not alone. We can find better ways to navigate life when we cooperate, when we find win-win deals, when we pay more attention to what other humans, other animals, other cells (or even organelles!) need. And who knows? Maybe we can come up with a cool-looking mitochondria analogue one day.

Footnote
[1] Horizontal gene transfer is a type of passage of genetic material, not to descendants, but between individual of different species directly. It's most often found in prokaryotes.
