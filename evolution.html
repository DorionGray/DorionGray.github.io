Evolution Does Not Have an End Goal

Maria Matias · August 2026 · 9 min read
↗
TL; DR:Erroneously admitting evolution has an end goal may create extreme amounts of suffering through speciesism.

It’s very common to refer to a species as ’more evolved’ than another, especially when comparing two beings very different from each other, not only in physiology but also in environment. And that's a rather intuitive thought, I believe. When we see a human, with trillions of cells cooperating, renovating, organising into dozens of different systems that differ immensely from one another but which are deeply interwined, and then we see a sponge who has a literal hole serving as both mouth and anus, we are pretty driven to think evolution has given us some upper hand and that we must be at least a bit more ‘evolved’ than those, right?

But when we refer to something similar to such ideas, we carry a very nuanced misconception: we are implying a ladder, some end goal of maximum evolution to be achieved, but that’s quite far from reality. No being is more evolved than another because, inherently, there is no scale of evolution. We can indeed have beings with a larger genome, or more complex systems or even larger in size, but none of these metrics indicates ‘evolution degrees’, because, once again, there are none.

To truly understand the degree to which this affirmation is misleading, we need to take a quick step back to look at how natural evolution actually happens.

Little disclaimer: I will barely touch the surface of this subject here, as my main goal is to just give my thoughts on the true impact of this misconception and not really give an overview of evolutionary biology, which is an amazingly complex, intertwined and passionate field which I advise you to explore more in depth.

With that out of the way, let's get the basics down: we've all heard the term natural selection. It's a term that's often thrown around without much meaning in our daily lives and, overall, outside of actual scientific activities. Natural selection occurs because individual organisms, even from the same species, are different from one another. There's always some variation within species. A very stupid example I like to give is the colour of cats: in their genomes, cats vary in what pigments their fur presents and therefore we can get tons of different cute pets!

These variations in the genome are often called mutations, and most of the time these variations are random and even accidental. They may result in just neutral alterations (I highly doubt my sister's hair being slightly lighter than mine will give her much advantage), but sometimes, those alterations actually increase an individual's capability of surviving in their environment.

Imagine you're an herbivore quadruped in the savanna, for example. There really isn't much food down on the ground, but there are some cool leaves up there on trees. If you're an individual with a slightly longer neck than others, then you'll probably have easier access to food, which does both (i) decrease your chances of, well, starving to death, and (ii) make you look pretty healthy and a nice mating partner to spread out your nice tall genes.

And thus, you're more likely to have descendants than your short counterparts because you have an advantage that helps you in your specific environment. And thus, giraffes have some really big necks. These random mutations that actually help individuals are advantages because they allow an organism to better survive in their environment, and if you are more likely to survive, you're more likely to reproduce. And that increased likelihood to reproduce is the main driver of natural selection. In fact, some mutations do not increase your life expectancy, but they do increase your likelihood of just reproducing (like having some cool feathers as a peacock to impress the ladies).

All in all, when a certain mutation gives any kind of advantage, it's more likely to be passed on, and so, it becomes more common in a population's genome pool. Isn't that cool? As the environment changes, different mutations become more useful, so they are passively ‘selected’. That's where the common expression ‘survival of the fittest’ comes from. In nature, organisms have a feedback loop of survival between generations. Not some kind of design. Useful things get passed on through generations. That also means harmful mutations are likely to disappear from a population because if you die early, you won't pass much of your genome on, and your characteristics will probably end with you. (Which is why smaller giraffes probably disappeared from the population in the savanna).

So, practically, evolution isn't designed; it isn't actively trying to achieve more complex, or impressive or ‘smart’ organisms. Most of these things come from a very passive, almost random process, which is far from designed or even driven. It’s just a feedback loop, where the driver is sheer survival.

But if we look throughout history, we will notice that this idea is extremely counterintuitive to us. Several religions have outright rejected the whole idea of evolution when it was first introduced (although it was more to do with dogmas and power games rather than evolution itself), choosing the idea of divine intervention, with humans as the epitome of all beings (this is a huge generalisation! There’s an enormous variety of religious and cultural belief which offer a spectrum of ideas on evolution and origin of different species). This is not to show that religions are incorrect or misleading: I just want to exemplify that early thinkers, moved mostly by intuition, considered the idea that changes just happen really, really poor when compared to a purposeful and well-thought-out design.

Even today, we use the terms ‘evolution selects’ and ‘to be more evolved’ which, as we’ve seen, are pretty much completely incorrect. Why is it so hard for us to understand that evolution just happens? Without end goals, without a scale, just a passive and natural process. Well, humans are extremely goal-oriented: literally, biologically goal-oriented, so much so that dopamine is released when we make progress or achieve a goal. Historically, goal-setting enabled us to face challenges and difficulties more easily.

>

Our capacity for abstract thinking has also reinforced the nature of goal-setting: visualising futures that do not yet exist requires a significant level of abstraction and, in fact, most of our goals are abstract in themselves, such as becoming a better person or helping the world! As best we can, we mirror our own characteristics into the processes we see. Evolution often (but definitely not always) results in ever slightly more complex organisms (here complexity may be taken from different proxies, from genome size to behaviour complexity or just the amount of structures and systems an individual presents). At first glance, evolution seems to be coming up gradually with organisms that seem to have a pattern of increased complexity, and that is extremely similar to our own goal-achieving process. We are looking to gradually improve some aspect of our universe to achieve something more complex than the path of least resistance. From that, I believe it’s pretty easy to extrapolate a goal from the process of natural selection.

But you may be asking, and rightfully so: ‘OK, so what’s the big deal? What’s intrinsically bad about thinking that evolution is driven to something? Isn’t it nice to have an easier way to understand the process of natural selection?’

Think of it for a moment: this misconception we’ve been exploring for the last few paragraphs carries a very harmful assumption: that there are more evolved beings than others. Ignoring the fact that individuals are the way they are because that’s literally the result of natural passive progression without any end goal to be attained, we gain the idea that there’s an absolute level of perfection to be achieved, and if that exists, then those who have achieved it, which are ‘more evolved ’, are biologically superior to others down the ladder. If we assume that evolution is a process which is striving for something (which already erroneously sees it as an entity), we must accept that some beings are already closer to perfection or, at least, to the ‘end goal’. And that creates bias. When we think of beings as more evolved, more capable, we are driven to take a closer consideration of the needs, wants and pleasures of those beings who we deem, arbitrarily, are less evolved than we or other species are.

There’s a name for this kind of bias: it’s called speciesism. It was a term coined back in the 1970’s and, as the name suggests, it refers to the discrimination in treatment of animals due only to species membership. Speciesism is incredibly harmful, especially because most of us don’t really take it seriously. But if you open up your mind a bit and admit that animals are also capable of feeling suffering, then we begin to understand just how much speciesism actually affects our world. This kind of discrimination leads us to be extremely disconnected from the suffering and pain of other living beings. Just imagine how many animals are in the world: how many of those are tortured while being farmed in intensive facilities, how many live lives of pure suffering in the wild. Take all of that into account and just weigh how much more suffering is being caused because we simply don’t take animal suffering into consideration. It sounds absolutely bogus, right?

Speciesism is an extremely difficult and rather hard and ingrained issue to tackle, and the evolution misconception we mentioned is definitely not the only or even the biggest barrier to facing this kind of discrimination. But I believe it is important to consider, and is especially useful to help us understand that some of our actions, which have an extremely large impact, are truly just a result of misconceptions and of not thinking hard enough about what kind of suffering we are to take on. Imagine, for example, a world where no species is considered ‘more evolved’ than the next. What would that mean? Well, maybe not much, but I really do believe that it would at least make us more accountable for understanding other species, to be more empathetic and to have a more rational and purposeful approach to treating all the other beings with whom we share the world

Ultimately, evolution makes no being superior to another; we’re all just products of iteratively trying to survive the best we can through generations and generations, continuously adapting to our environment. And that’s really all there is to it, which may even help you think a bit more deeply on how you think about the other organisms that live alongside us.
